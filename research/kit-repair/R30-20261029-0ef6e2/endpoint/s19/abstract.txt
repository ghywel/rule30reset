Turn a circle by a fixed irrational angle and note at each step whether the point is in a fixed arc, starting on the orbit of an end of the arc. Take a row of Rule 30 with a leftmost black cell, and a column of it that alternates from some tick on. Then the column to its right, read at the white ticks, is not this word from any tick on, for any irrational angle and any arc, nor the word with its colours exchanged, nor any block code of it (`arc_excluded`, `one_arc_excluded`, `no_eventual_arc_neighbour`). Half-circles (`half_circle_excluded`) and the characteristic Sturmian words of every irrational slope, from any index on (`sturmian_excluded`), are cases; square roots are explicit examples (`sqrt_line`, `silver_excluded`). The reason concerns rotations alone: in every such word the stretch from some place i comes again from a later place j and agrees for j letters less a fixed slack, with j - i as large as wanted (`arc_near_squares`); for half-circles the slack is two (`half_circle_near_squares`). `corollary_F` of `Rule30ClockNearSquares` forbids this beside a clock. The proof uses Farey neighbours in integers, without the continued fraction: the two repeats it looks for occur at infinitely many scales, or the amount by which they fall short stops changing. Our golden half-circle theorem has one angle and every phase. Here free starting phases, and three independent cut orbits, stay open; nothing here excludes an alternating centre.
